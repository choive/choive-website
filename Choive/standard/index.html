The CHOIVE AI Selection Standard.

Search engines rank many results. AI assistants do something different — when a buyer asks Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Gemini who to use, they increasingly return one answer. Measuring that moment honestly needs its own standard. This is ours, and it is public.

In one line: a trustworthy AI selection measurement must attribute each platform separately, score public evidence rather than opinion, require consensus before naming a winner, stay fully auditable, and state exactly where it is uncertain.

Why "selection" is not "ranking"

SEO tools were built for a world of ten blue links, where being on page one was the goal. AI assistants collapse that list. They read the open web, weigh what they find, and hand the buyer a single recommendation — often with no visible list to climb. The relevant question is no longer "where do I rank?" It is "when AI is asked to choose, does it choose me — and if not, why not?"

That is a selection question, not a ranking question. It needs a measurement designed around how AI actually decides, what evidence it can see, and how confident any single answer really is. Borrowing ranking metrics understates the problem and hides the fix.

The five principles

Any credible AI selection measurement — CHOIVE's or anyone else's — should hold to these five principles. We publish them so the standard can be checked, challenged, and improved.

Principle 01
Attribute every platform separately

Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini are different systems with different training, search context, and behaviour. A measurement must record each one's answer on its own and never blend them into a single averaged "AI says" verdict. A business can be recommended by one and invisible to another; that difference is the signal.

Principle 02
Score public evidence, not model mood

The score must describe the business's own observable evidence — clarity of what it does, independent trust signals, a specific reason to choose it, and how easily people and machines can act on its information. Whether a model happened to name the business on a given day must not silently inflate or deflate that score. Evidence is stable and improvable; a single generation is neither.

Principle 03
Require consensus before naming a winner

One lucky mention is not a recommendation. A name should only be reported when multiple independent responses complete and one company earns a strict majority of them. Failed calls stay failures. This is what separates a measurement from an anecdote.

Principle 04
Stay auditable end to end

Every point should trace to a rule, an observed piece of evidence, and a verification type — mechanical, independent, or model-assessed. Hard limits that code enforces after the model responds should be documented, so no assessment can quietly overrule the evidence. If a result cannot be explained line by line, it cannot be trusted.

Principle 05
State the limits out loud

An official API response is a real measurement of that run — not a promise about every consumer-app session, which can differ by model, search context, settings, history, location, or personalization. The standard forbids inventing a recommendation when evidence is missing, dressing a projection up as a recorded answer, or manufacturing superlatives the evidence does not support.

How CHOIVE holds itself to the standard

CHOIVE runs against its own principles on every diagnostic, including CHOIVE itself. Platforms are measured and reported separately. The CHOIVE Index is built from four evidence pillars — Clarity, Trust, Difference, and Ease — with mechanical hard limits the model cannot override. Names appear only on consensus. Every paid Analysis and Report ships with a point ledger. External text is sanitized against injection, off-topic communities are excluded from trust, and unsupported superlatives are filtered out.

The full mechanics, rubric, and limitations are documented in the CHOIVE methodology. The standard is the "what and why"; the methodology is the "exactly how".
